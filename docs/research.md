# Research: Alternatives Students Use Today

Checked October 2026. These are the three channels named in the brief: an Arab aggregator, Telegram channels, and official databases. For the official databases, the DAAD scholarship database (Germany) is used as a representative example.

## 1. Forsa (for9a.com)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who it is for** | Arab youth looking for scholarships, internships, volunteering, competitions, jobs, and free courses. Content is in Arabic and English. |
| **What it does well** | A very large catalogue. The site claims 1,000+ open opportunities, 100+ new ones each week, and 5.2M+ registered users. It filters by degree level (Master's, PhD), funding type (fully or partially funded), country, and specialty, including *Computer and Information Sciences* and *Information Technology*. Each listing shows how many days are left to apply and links to the official application page. It also has an app, a Telegram channel, articles, and free courses. |
| **What it misses** | It's built for discovery, not for applying. It covers every field, so AI/IT students still filter through a lot of unrelated listings (art residencies, humanitarian programs). It has no personal workspace for storing CVs or motivation letters, and no help writing them (based on the public site; features behind a login were not checked). |
| **What it costs** | Free. Forsa states it charges users no fees and earns from advertising and business partnerships. |

## 2. Telegram scholarship channels

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who it is for** | Arab students who want opportunities pushed to their phone. There are many channels, from Forsa's own channel to independent ones run by individuals. |
| **What it does well** | Fast and mobile. New posts arrive as notifications, often in Arabic, and students already use the app every day. |
| **What it misses** | No search or filtering by field, degree, or deadline. Old posts get buried and there's no way to save or track an opportunity except forwarding it or copying the link. Quality varies by channel, and posts often summarise or repost from other sources rather than linking to the official page. Nothing helps with documents or letters. |
| **What it costs** | Free to join. Some channels promote paid services such as consultations or "application packages." (Not verified for specific channels. Worth checking during student interviews.) |

## 3. Official university and funder databases (example: DAAD)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who it is for** | International students and researchers looking for funding in one specific country or institution. DAAD covers Germany and mostly targets graduates, doctoral candidates, and postdocs. |
| **What it does well** | The most authoritative source: requirements, funding amounts, and deadlines come straight from the funder. DAAD's database lists around 160 to 180 options, with filters for status group (student, graduate, PhD, postdoc) and subject area, and each program page includes the application procedure and dates. |
| **What it misses** | Each portal covers only its own country or institution, so students have to visit many portals with different layouts. That is exactly the tab overload described in the brief. A program listed may be closed for the current round. No personal workspace and no help with letters. |
| **What it costs** | Free. |

## Key finding

All three alternatives are **free**, and Forsa already filters by IT and Computer Science and shows deadlines. So the brief's claim that existing sites paywall public information is **not supported** for the main alternative, and "free listings filtered by field" alone is not a differentiator. What none of the three offer is the **application side**: storing a student's letters and CVs in one place and helping draft them for a specific opportunity.

**Why use this platform instead:** Forsa and Telegram help you *find* an opportunity. This platform keeps the AI-focused shortlist, your motivation letters, and your CV together, and helps you write the letter for that specific scholarship.
